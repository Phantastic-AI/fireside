# The story

Every conference organizer we have ever met keeps a second system of
record: the spreadsheet of shame. The real tool has the submissions in it,
but the spreadsheet knows the truth — who has actually been told, who is
being swapped in if the keynote cancels, which speaker still owes slides
and which one it would be rude to chase. The tool records; the spreadsheet
*remembers who has been told what.* That gap is where conferences get
hurt: the acceptance email that went out twice, the rejection that never
went out at all, the speaker who learned their fate from the published
agenda.

Fireside is built on one sentence: **a decision leaves when you send it,
not when you make it. Who has been told is never a guess.** Deciding
stages a letter; the outbox holds it; a person reads the number, confirms
that number, and the letters go — all of them, once, with the committee's
own words inside. The count on the screen is a database query, not a hope.
Everything else in the product radiates from that spine: the portal that
stays honest by staying silent, the pile whose filter chips are the
counts, the reviewer whose score is hers until she submits it.

The second belief is that speakers are people who are about to do
something generous and slightly terrifying, and software should treat
them accordingly. The form teaches by example instead of gatekeeping. A
half-written abstract survives a dead battery. A rejection arrives in
plain words with a future in it. The portal never says "status:
IN_REVIEW" — it says "With the committee," because that is the sentence a
person would say.

The third is that a conference does not end when the chairs are stacked.
Talks were given; recordings exist; the people you met in corridors are
the reason you came. So a finished conference stays published with its
recordings on the session pages, a speaker's history follows them across
years and events, and an attendee's starred schedule can — only ever by
their own choice — become a way to find the people who were going to the
same rooms.

And because this is 2026: every door a person can walk through, an agent
can too. The MCP endpoint is not a bolt-on API — it is the same workflows,
the same guards, the same refusal sentences. When the call is closed, it
is closed to everyone politely.

We are building this in the open for the AI Engineer *Kill My SaaS*
challenge, aimed at one very specific dream customer: the conference we
keep coming back to. Fireside is one of the faces this engine wants to
wear — the same object model underneath runs any gathering where people
propose, someone decides, and everyone needs to know where to stand at
9:30 on Thursday.
